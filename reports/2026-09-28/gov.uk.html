Context

GOV.UK is the UK government's single website. It launched on 17 October 2012, replacing Directgov and Business Link, and now also hosts the publishing of every ministerial department and most agencies (Wikipedia). The Government Digital Service (GDS), part of the Department for Science, Innovation and Technology, runs it. It has no business model: it is taxpayer-funded, carries no advertising and exists to deflect phone calls and paper forms onto digital guidance and services. For an index builder it is the opposite of the usual top site. Content is Crown copyright under the Open Government Licence v3.0, which allows commercial reuse with attribution. Robots.txt blocks almost nothing. And the same JSON that renders every page is published as a documented, keyless Content API. The hard part is not access but shape. The corpus is dominated by long-tail legal and administrative records (tribunal decisions, HMRC manuals, permit notices), not the roughly 80K pages of citizen guidance people actually search for. Sitemap lastmod also tracks internal republishing, not real change.

Retrieval Notes (site-specific deltas)

Only where gov.uk differs from the default platform assumptions.

Every default is inverted

No login and no personalisation on content pages. No AI-crawler blocks. The licence explicitly permits reuse, and GOV.UK's reuse page invites scraping within robots.txt. The only curated sliver is the Vertex-backed site search at /search/all, which is disallowed, and that is an output, not a source. The API and the rendered page share one JSON source, so there is no gap between what a crawler sees and what a user sees.

content_id is the join key

Each document has a UUID content_id that is stable across URL changes. It appears in the Content API, the search index and a govuk:content-id meta tag on every HTML page. Translations share the content_id and differ by locale. Organisations, taxons and people are themselves content items, so the links hash gives a typed graph (publisher → document → topic) without any NLP.

Use public_updated_at, not lastmod

72% of sitemap entries were "modified" in the last two months, because lastmod follows internal updated_at, which moves on template and schema republishes. Recrawling on lastmod means refetching about 600K unchanged pages. Instead, diff on public_updated_at through the Search API, and read details.change_history for the publisher's own change notes.

Rank the head, not the corpus

Employment tribunal decisions and HMRC manual sections alone are 31% of searchable documents, and 20% predate 2015 (calculated by Site Wise). Citizen queries ("vat rates", "renew passport", "ETA") land on a much smaller set of mainstream answer, guide, transaction and guidance pages. GDS itself grounds Chat on about 80K guidance pages. Weight on document_type and content_purpose_supergroup, and demote is_historic. Historic items are still live and are labelled on the page as published under a previous government.

The search index hides some objects

HTML publications (107K) and withdrawn items do not appear in /api/search.json, and the search index cannot filter by locale. An index built from search alone will miss about 18% of the sitemap (calculated by Site Wise). Use the sitemap for coverage and search for metadata and change detection.

Query profile

Short, task-shaped UK English queries ("tax disc", "council tax band", "universal credit login"), often pointing at a service on a different *.service.gov.uk host. The GOV.UK start page is the indexable object; the service behind it is not. Traffic spikes follow events such as budgets, storms and conflicts. Travel advice pages went from 1.5K to 54K weekly visits during the India–Pakistan tensions in May 2025, per the 2025 usage post.

Indexing Plan

  • Retrieval unit: the content item: content_id + locale, with body text, type, publishing organisation, taxons and dates. Attachments are child documents keyed by attachment id and media URL, with page count and ISBN where present.
  • Primary keys & joins: content_id ↔ base_path ↔ links.organisations/taxons/parent. HTML publications join to their parent through links.parent. Tribunal decisions carry case numbers in the slug and title.
  • Ingestion paths, ranked: 1) The Search API full listing (475 calls, about 15 minutes) for metadata and public_timestamp. 2) The sitemap for the 107K items search omits. 3) The Content API for bodies and links at ≤10 requests per second, about 24 hours for the full corpus. 4) Attachments from the assets host, PDFs first. 5) Atom feeds per organisation as a low-latency trigger.
  • Recrawl cadence: poll search order=-public_timestamp every 15 minutes, since there are about 300 changes a day. Take the sitemap nightly to detect additions and deletions only. Re-fetch mainstream pages on public_updated_at change. Leave tribunal decisions and manuals effectively immutable after ingest.
  • Derivatives to generate: PDF text and tables (statistics attachments are the main source of numeric facts); a type-aware quality prior; PII redaction or down-ranking for tribunal decisions; Welsh–English parallel pairs from available_translations.
  • Demo to build: a "what changed in government this week" feed. Diff public_updated_at through the Search API, show each item's change_history note, and group by organisation. It uses only documented, OGL-licensed endpoints.

Risks to the Index

  • The Search API is labelled unsupported. Site search has already moved to search-api-v2 on Vertex AI Search. If the legacy Elasticsearch index is retired, the fast metadata listing and public_timestamp diffing go with it. Keep the sitemap plus Content API path working on its own.
  • Personal data in legal records. The 150K+ tribunal decisions name claimants and respondents. The OGL does not cover personal data, so an index needs a policy for name queries and for removal requests.
  • Mixed licensing inside attachments. OGL applies "except where otherwise stated". Ordnance Survey maps, third-party photos and externally hosted attachments (for example digital.nhs.uk or ons.gov.uk links) need their own checks before display or redistribution.
  • Authority is also a liability. Answers drawn from GOV.UK are read as official. Stale or historic pages must be served with their dates and withdrawn_notice, or the index will confidently give out superseded rules. Tax thresholds, for example, change every April.

Open Questions

  • The total number of attachment files and their bytes. Could be closed by walking details.attachments for every publication-type content item (about 245K parent publications, statistics and consultations in the sitemap, so about 7 hours at 10 requests per second; calculated by Site Wise) and summing file_size.
  • How many Welsh .cy translations exist. The sitemap has none and search rejects filter_locale. Could be closed by counting available_translations with locale: cy during the Content API pass.
  • How many withdrawn items exist and whether they stay fetchable. Could be closed by diffing the sitemap against search and checking withdrawn_notice in the Content API for the residual URLs that are not HTML publications (about 51K).
  • Whether the 10 requests per second limit is enforced by 429 or by queueing. The docs say requests "won't be processed" and no rate headers were seen. Could be closed with a controlled burst test at 12–15 requests per second for 60 seconds.
  • Whether GOV.UK exposes a public bulk export or change stream, such as Publishing API events, as an alternative to polling. Could be closed by reviewing docs.publishing.service.gov.uk and asking GDS through the API support contact.
