Context

Encyclopædia Britannica, Inc. is a privately held Chicago reference publisher, founded 1768, that ended its print edition in 2012 and now runs an ad-supported free web encyclopedia, a premium membership, a school/library subscription business, and Merriam-Webster. Its corpus is entirely editorially commissioned and signed — no user-generated layer, and so none of the trust-tiering most platforms require. That provenance is what makes it attractive as a grounding corpus for answer engines, and Britannica has responded by suing one: it filed against Perplexity in September 2025 over verbatim copying and hallucinated attribution. For an index builder the site is unusual twice over. Its crawl surface is closed by infrastructure rather than policy — robots.txt is permissive and enforcement lives entirely in Cloudflare (Probe Log). And its licensed path is genuinely good: a syndication API with an incremental lastUpdated filter, stable numeric article IDs, and the same topic published at five reading levels.

Retrieval Notes (site-specific deltas)

Only where Britannica differs from the default platform assumptions.

Policy and enforcement have separated

The methodology's default is that robots.txt blocks AI crawlers. Britannica inverts it: robots.txt names no AI agent and allows ~20 ad-tech crawlers, while Cloudflare 403s everything — including Googlebot's UA and the sitemap robots.txt itself advertises. inferred The allow-list is an ad-verification whitelist, not a crawl policy; crawl policy has moved into Cloudflare bot management, where it is unpublishable and unauditable. Basis: every named agent is a brand-safety or ad-verification vendor (Grapeshot, IAS_crawler, Peer39_crawler, DoubleVerifyBot, Proximic, TTD-Content), no editorial or search agent appears, and the origin returns cf-mitigated: challenge to all four UAs tested.

Three article counts, three denominators

87K+ (API, full product), 130K+ (marketing), 255.8K (/topic/ sitemap URLs). They are not in conflict: the API counts licensable article records, marketing counts web articles including non-syndicated verticals, and the sitemap counts URLs including redirect aliases and sub-topic pages. Pick the denominator that matches your ingestion path and never mix them in a coverage metric.

Do not trust lastmod

All 5,280 sampled sitemap lastmod values are one build stamp (Probe Log). Any recrawl scheduler keyed on it will either refetch the entire 307K corpus every build or never refetch at all. The real signals are JSON-LD dateModified on the page and the API's lastUpdated query parameter — the latter is the only one that supports a server-side delta.

Retrieval unit: section, not article

Article length spans 255 to 131,706 words. /place/France as one document is useless for retrieval and would dominate any BM25 or embedding index. The API's XML carries an explicit h1–h6 hierarchy, so the natural unit is the section, keyed articleId + section path, with the article as the parent record.

Cross-modal joins

articleId is the spine; media attach through <assembly> and <inlinemedia> elements inside the article body, so image–text association is authored rather than inferred — no OCR or co-occurrence heuristics needed. The Wikidata QID joins Britannica to the wider entity graph, and contributor IDs join articles to signed experts, a provenance edge no UGC site can offer.

No spam surface, but a staleness surface

With no user-generated content and no incentive to game ranking, trust tiering is unnecessary. The quality risk is inverted: an editorially-maintained corpus decays quietly. /technology/API is 255 words last modified 2026-03-16; /event/World-War-II is 46K words touched 2026-08-15. Depth tracks editorial priority, not traffic.

Reading levels are a real asset

Five parallel renderings of one topic is something almost no other corpus in this series offers. It supports audience-conditioned retrieval and yields free supervised pairs for summarization and simplification — provided the licence covers derivative training, which it does not obviously do.

Query profile

Definitional and entity-lookup: "what is X", "who was X", "when did X happen" — precisely the class that answer engines absorb before a click happens. That substitution is the injury pleaded against Perplexity and the reason the crawl wall exists.

Indexing Plan

  • Retrieval unit: article section (from the API XML heading hierarchy), keyed articleId + section path; the article record is the parent, carrying title, contributors, category, Wikidata QID and dateModified.
  • Primary keys & joins: articleId internally; Wikidata QID for external entity resolution; contributorId for provenance; articleTypeId to align reading-level variants of the same topic; media by CDN path.
  • Ingestion paths, ranked: 1) Syndication API — the only sanctioned bulk path; /articles?articleTypeId=&lastUpdated= paginates 1,000 per page and gives true incremental deltas, but the free tier's 1,000 queries/day and two-product cap means a full 87K-article pull is a licensing conversation, not an engineering one. 2) Wikidata QID join — free, unlicensed, and lets you place Britannica in your entity graph before you have any text rights at all. 3) cdn.britannica.com — technically open for media, but rights are per-asset and frequently third-party; hotlink, do not mirror. 4) Public crawl — closed. Every UA tested gets a Cloudflare 403; budget nothing for it.
  • Recrawl cadence: poll /articles?lastUpdated={last_run} daily per licensed product — cheap, server-side, and the whole reason to prefer the API. Ignore sitemap lastmod entirely. Re-resolve Wikidata mappings monthly; treat media as immutable by path.
  • Derivatives to generate: section-level embeddings; a reading-level alignment table (same topic × 5 levels) as summarization training pairs, licence permitting; a contributor-authority table; a Britannica↔Wikipedia coverage diff keyed on QID, which is directly useful as an editorial gap-finder for both sides.
  • Demo to build: a lastmod liar checker — feed it a sitemap index, compare declared lastmod against JSON-LD dateModified on a sample of its URLs, and report the fraction of the corpus whose freshness signal is a build stamp. Britannica scores 0% useful; the interesting output is running it across the sites already profiled in this series.

Risks to the Index

  • The wall is a dial, not a door. Enforcement lives in Cloudflare bot management, so Britannica can change who gets through without editing a single published policy file. An index built on any residual crawl access can lose it silently and with no diffable signal.
  • Litigation defines the licence terms. Britannica's public position on AI grounding is a lawsuit (S.D.N.Y. 1:25-cv-07546), and no AI licensing programme is published on the developer portal. The free tier's non-commercial limit plus case-by-case commercial terms means an AI-facing index has no priced path today — only a negotiation whose outcome the pending motion to dismiss will influence.
  • inferred The free API tier is not designed for corpus ingestion. Basis: 1,000 queries/day against an 87K-article product is a ~87-day full pull even before media, and the two-reference-API cap blocks the reading-level ladder that is the corpus's most distinctive feature.
  • Media rights are per-asset and unstated. The CDN is open (Probe Log) but captions carry third-party credits; the open CDN is an availability fact, not a licence, and mistaking one for the other is the easiest way to acquire liability here.
  • Ownership is in motion. A confidentially-filed IPO has been reported since 2024 and has not completed. A public listing or a sale would likely re-price syndication and could either open a formal AI licence or harden the wall further.
