Context

The Chrome Web Store is Google's catalogue of extensions, themes and the remaining Chrome apps. It opened on 7 December 2010 (Wikipedia) and moved from chrome.google.com/webstore to its own host with a redesign in November 2023 (9to5Google). The old paths now 301 to the new host. It is not a revenue line in itself: the store sells nothing and carries no ads. Its job is to control what code runs inside Chrome. For an index builder it is unusual in two ways. First, the listing page is a thin shell over a rich record. The SSR payload carries the full manifest.json (permissions, host access, content-script matches), exact rating averages and counts, creation and update timestamps, the developer's trader status and privacy disclosures. Second, the object itself, the CRX package, can be fetched through Chrome's public update protocol, so the corpus includes executable code as well as metadata. The hard part is the shape of the catalogue. Most listings have single-digit users, zero ratings and are less than two years old, so ranking matters more than coverage.

Google publishes almost nothing about the store. Corpus figures come from Site Wise probes on 2026-09-29. Sample shares carry wide error bars: ±7 points at n=194 (calculated by Site Wise).

Sitemap corpus
368,367 unique /detail/ URLs across 44 shards. There are no category, developer or collection URLs and no lastmod. Probe Log verified
Dead listings in the sitemap
6 of 200 sampled IDs (3%) render a soft-404 (/detail/empty-title/{id}, HTTP 200). That puts the live corpus at about 357K (calculated by Site Wise). Probe Log verified
Item mix
Of the 194 live samples, 42 (22%) are themes, which puts them at about 80K (calculated by Site Wise). 143 of the 152 non-themes are Manifest V3. The 9 MV2 items are mostly legacy Chrome apps with no category. Probe Log verified
User distribution
The median is 9.5 users. 76% show fewer than 100, 94% fewer than 1,000 and 1% have 10K or more. 27% show no user count at all. Exstats puts the median Chrome extension at 14 users (Exstats). Probe Log verified
Ratings coverage
59% of sampled listings have zero ratings. An earlier study found no meaningful difference between the median ratings of benign and malicious extensions (Hsu, Tran & Fass 2024). Probe Log verified
Churn
42% of sampled listings were created in the last 12 months and 52% were updated in that time. 12% have not been updated for more than 4 years. Exstats counted a net +43,418 extensions in Q2 2026 alone (Exstats). Probe Log verified
Package availability
Of 1,000 random sitemap IDs sent to the update endpoint, 979 returned a CRX and 21 returned noupdate. The 4 we checked were all MV2 listings. Probe Log verified
Package bytes
The median CRX is 228KB, the 90th percentile 3.7MB and the mean 1.9MB. The full package corpus is about 684GB (368K × 97.9% × mean; calculated by Site Wise). Probe Log verified
Localization
87% of sitemap entries list a single hreflang, and 86% of sampled listings declare one language. The sitemap uses 69 locale codes in all. Probe Log verified
Security-noteworthy installs
More than 346M users installed a malicious, policy-violating or vulnerable extension in the study window (July 2020 to May 2023). Malware stayed in the store a median of about 380 days. Only about 60% of extensions were still listed a year after publication. Hsu, Tran & Fass 2024 verified

Retrieval Notes (site-specific deltas)

Only where chromewebstore.google.com differs from the default platform assumptions.

Open by default, sub-pages walled

Unlike most Google properties, the store has no AI-crawler blocks, no login wall and no JavaScript gate. The whole SEO surface is the detail page. The walls are narrow and deliberate: reviews, privacy, support, related and search are disallowed, which keeps user text and query pages out of third-party indexes while leaving every listing open.

The ID is a public key hash

The 32-character [a-p] ID is derived from the developer's signing key, so it is stable for the life of the item and across Chrome, enterprise policies and the update protocol. The slug in the URL changes with the name and is ignored. Canonicalize to /detail/{id}. Removed items keep resolving with HTTP 200 to /detail/empty-title/{id}, so test for that slug rather than the status code.

The manifest is the authoritative field

Titles and descriptions are SEO-tuned, often keyword-stuffed ("YouTube AdBlock – …"). The manifest declares what the item can actually do: permissions, host_permissions, content_scripts.matches, externally_connectable. It is exposed without downloading the package. Retrieval for "extensions that can read my Gmail" should rank on manifest host patterns, not on description text.

Change detection through the update protocol

The sitemap has no lastmod, but the update endpoint returns version and hash_sha256 for 100 IDs per call. A daily sweep of the whole catalogue costs about 3.7K requests (calculated by Site Wise), and only changed IDs need their detail pages re-fetched. User counts and ratings drift without a version bump, so re-fetch pages on a slower schedule.

The trust signal lives off the page

With a median of about 10 users and 59% of listings unrated, engagement signals are absent for most of the corpus. Two usable priors: the Featured flag (7% of the sample), which Google says is being retired later in 2026; and _esbAllowlist in the update response (67%). inferred Basis: _esbAllowlist is Enhanced Safe Browsing's trusted-extension flag, because it is true on established items such as uBlock Origin Lite and Twitch Adblock and is false on a third of random IDs.

Query profile

Queries are functional and brand-led: "ad blocker", "dark mode", "grammarly", "json viewer", "pdf editor". The long tail is enterprise kiosk apps and single-school tools (for example "ccsd iready kiosk app"), with anime and game themes making up most of the theme corpus (the top sitemap slugs are theme names). Search needs strong deduplication of clones: the related list for uBO Lite alone contains several near-identical "AdGuard" and "uBlock" variants.

Indexing Plan

  • Retrieval unit: the item version: id + version. It carries the listing text, the category, users, the exact rating and count, timestamps, the developer ID and trader flag, the manifest, and CRX hash and size. Screenshots and the promo video are attached media.
  • Primary keys & joins: id ↔ CRX hash_sha256 ↔ developer ID. Manifest host_permissions and content_scripts.matches give a join from each extension to the sites it touches, which is a cross-site edge no other store exposes. Clone detection works by hashing the files inside the CRX.
  • Ingestion paths, ranked: 1) The sitemap nightly (44 shards, about 20MB gzip) for the ID universe and additions or deletions. 2) An update-protocol sweep (about 3.7K calls) for version and hash change detection. 3) Detail pages for changed IDs only, parsing ds:0; the first pass is about 235GB. 4) CRX downloads for items above a user threshold (at 1K users that is about 6% of the corpus from the sample), for code features. 5) Category pages for Google's editorial ordering.
  • Recrawl cadence: the sitemap and the update sweep daily. Detail pages on version change, plus a 30-day refresh for users and ratings. Themes and pre-2022 items quarterly.
  • Derivatives to generate: a permission-risk score from the manifest; a site→extension graph from host patterns; code-level similarity clusters over CRX contents; OCR of screenshots for UI-described features; and embeddings of descriptions deduplicated by cluster.
  • Demo to build: a "which extensions can touch this site" lookup. Enter a domain and get every listed extension whose manifest host patterns match it, ranked by users, built entirely from sitemap plus ds:0 manifests.

Risks to the Index

  • The positional ds:0 schema is undocumented. Field indexes such as [14] for users and [18] for the manifest can shift with any front-end release. Validate field types on every run, and fall back to the visible-text parse and the CRX manifest.
  • The update protocol is Chrome's plumbing, not a data API. It is keyless now, but bulk use by a non-browser could be throttled or require client attestation. Keep request volume near what a single Chrome profile with many extensions would generate, and cache hashes.
  • The corpus is being reshaped by policy. MV2 listings were due to be removed on 2026-08-31 (MV2 timeline), yet MV2 residue is still listed. A new default cap of 2 published extensions per developer and the retirement of the Featured badge were announced on 2026-08-20 (CWS blog). Expect deletions in bulk and a slower rate of new items; keep tombstones.
  • Malicious items are a retrieval-safety problem. Malware has stayed listed for about a year on median (Verified Metrics). Ranking on ratings or users alone surfaces it. Removed items vanish into soft-404s, so the index must propagate deletions within a day.

Open Questions

  • The rate limit on detail pages and the update protocol. Could be closed by a controlled ramp from 1 to 5 requests per second over 10 minutes on each host, watching for 429s or CAPTCHA pages.
  • The exact theme, extension and app split, and the total count of reviews. Could be closed by a full ds:0 pass (manifest theme key, category path, rating count per ID); the review text total is not reachable without the disallowed path.
  • Whether the MV2 residue (about 2% of IDs) will be deleted. Could be closed by re-running the update-protocol census on the same 1,000 IDs monthly and diffing noupdate and soft-404 counts.
  • How many unlisted or private items exist outside the sitemap. Could be closed by feeding IDs from public sources (GitHub update_url references, enterprise policy docs) into the update protocol and counting ok responses for IDs not in the sitemap.
  • What _esbAllowlist means precisely. Could be closed by matching it against Chrome's Enhanced Safe Browsing "not trusted" install warning on a set of true and false IDs in a real Chrome profile.
