Context

nintendo.com is now the single web domain for Nintendo's regional companies. Japan moved from nintendo.co.jp, Europe from country domains such as nintendo.co.uk, and Australia followed; the old domains redirect. Each region still runs its own stack behind one Fastly front door: the Americas store is a Next.js app, Europe an older AEM/AngularJS site, Australia a Next.js app on Vercel, and Japan, Korea and Hong Kong static pages on S3. The site sells digital Switch and Switch 2 software (the eShop catalog), plus hardware and merchandise in the US, and publishes news, Nintendo Direct pages and support articles. For an index builder it is the authoritative game-catalog entity source: every eShop title has a stable 14-digit nsuid, ratings, publisher, genres, prices and trailer files. Both regional search backends answer anonymous queries. Against that, robots.txt carries a formal text-and-data-mining opt-out and blocks every named AI crawler.

Retrieval Notes (site-specific deltas)

Only where nintendo.com differs from the default platform assumptions.

One domain, four corpora

The domain merge is cosmetic for an indexer. Americas, Europe, Japan/Asia and Australia have different URL grammars, IDs, schemas and discovery paths. Europe publishes no sitemap at all; its open Solr index is the manifest. Treat each region as a separate source and join the regions on nsuid.

Identifier & dedup

nsuid (14 digits) is the platform key, and its prefix encodes the object class. In US slugs, 7005… appears exactly 10,926 times, matching the Algolia count of individual DLC; 7007… (831) marks bundles and passes; 7001… marks games. In the Americas it appears in nsuid, as the last 10 digits of sku, in Cloudinary paths, and in 11,814 of the 38,783 US slugs. Slugs collide across editions (…-switch, …-us, …-switch-2), so key on nsuid/sku, never the slug. Europe adds its own fs_id and the original platform code (playable_on_txt: HAC). 19,896 of its 23,616 game docs also carry nsuid_txt; the rest are mostly pre-Switch titles.

Crawler policy vs. reality

robots.txt is the strictest we have logged for a storefront: a blanket AI and CCBot ban plus a written TDM reservation. Yet the edge serves every UA the same 200, and both search backends are anonymous. The barrier is legal, not technical. Common Crawl honors the CCBot block (no store captures in 2026-39), so there is no third-party bulk copy to lean on.

An AI-readable channel by design

The single Allow for AI crawlers covers Nintendo Direct text versions: complete, bilingual transcripts of the company's main announcement format. inferred Nintendo intends AI assistants to answer "what was announced" from its own transcript rather than third-party summaries. Basis: the Allow: /jp/nintendo-direct/description/ line repeated in all eight AI-crawler groups, next to a dedicated sitemap for those pages.

Structured data is good but buggy

US product JSON-LD is unusually complete (VideoGame + Product + Offer + gallery). But the sampled VideoObject.contentUrl points to the image pipeline and 404s, and the description is SEO boilerplate ("Shop … at Nintendo Store"). Take the real description and trailer URLs from the Apollo Product record in __NEXT_DATA__.

Shovelware skews the catalog

38% of US objects cost under $5, and the top publishers by count are low-cost volume publishers, not Nintendo (518). inferred A count-weighted catalog index will be dominated by low-quality titles. Basis: the priceRange and softwarePublisher facet distributions. Use popularityRank / trendingRank (exposed in Algolia) and Europe's hits_i as quality priors.

Indexing Plan

  • Retrieval unit: the title entity, keyed by nsuid. It merges the per-region product records, attaches DLC (via parent), prices per country, ratings per board (ESRB, PEGI, CERO), media and Direct mentions.
  • Primary keys & joins: nsuid ↔ sku ↔ Cloudinary publicId ↔ Europe fs_id (via nsuid_txt where present, otherwise title + date). Direct transcript headings → title entities by name. YouTube video ID → Direct page.
  • Ingestion paths, ranked:
    1. Sitemaps (316,740 URLs), then product pages for the 9 Americas locales: ~287K page fetches at ~250KB each, about 72GB raw (calculated by Site Wise). Alternatively fetch only /us/ + /es-mx/ + /pt-br/ and use the price API for the remaining countries.
    2. Europe Solr partitioned below 1,000 rows (by type × system_type × year): 42K docs in roughly 50–100 requests.
    3. Algolia facet counts as a completeness check against the sitemaps. The US gap is 38,783 sitemap URLs vs 36,949 index objects; reconcile it.
    All three run against a written TDM opt-out. Treat this plan as requiring a rights decision (Risks) before any training-adjacent use.
  • Recrawl cadence: prices daily via the batched price API; new products and lastmod-dated news daily; full catalog weekly (US store lastmod is useless, so diff the URL set); Direct transcripts on each Direct.
  • Derivatives to generate: ASR is unnecessary for Directs (official transcripts exist); trailer keyframes and embeddings from the 720p MP4s; OCR is not needed.
  • Demo to build: a cross-region price and availability board. Join US Algolia nsuids to the price API for the 9 Americas countries and to Europe Solr, and flag titles missing from a region.

Risks to the Index

  • Explicit TDM reservation: the robots.txt comment invokes Article 4(3) of the EU DSM Directive and covers "web scraping … or any form of text or data mining". In the EU this is the machine-readable opt-out the law recognizes. Search indexing for retrieval and AI training need separate legal treatment, and Nintendo is a famously litigious rights holder.
  • Open backends can close without notice: the Algolia key ships in client JS and the Solr endpoint is undocumented. Either can be re-keyed or restricted by referrer at any time. Keep the sitemap and page path as the durable fallback.
  • Europe has no discovery fallback: no sitemap and no Common Crawl copy. If Solr closes, Europe falls back to link-crawling an AngularJS site.
  • Delistings: titles leave the eShop (Europe's eshop_removed_b flag). The index must keep tombstones, since removed titles stay notable as entities.
