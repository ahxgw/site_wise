Context

Chaturbate is a free-to-watch adult webcam site. Independent broadcasters stream live from home, and viewers buy tokens to tip them, pay for private shows, or join fan clubs. It launched in February 2011 and is run by Multi Media, LLC of Irvine, California, which is named in its terms. For an index builder it inverts the usual problem. The main object, a live stream, exists only while it is on air: the median live room on 2026-10-24 had been live for 1.7 hours. Everything durable sits around the stream: broadcaster profiles, room titles with hashtags, thumbnails, photo sets, follower counts and satisfaction votes. The site exposes all of this as unauthenticated JSON to every user agent, including AI crawlers. But its terms forbid "any automated means to scrape … including … crawlers", so the sanctioned third-party path is the affiliate program, not the open endpoints. The live corpus is also heavily concentrated: 43% of live rooms list Colombia as their country.

Retrieval Notes (site-specific deltas)

Only where chaturbate.com differs from the default platform assumptions.

Open to crawlers, closed by contract

The default picture is a curated crawler sliver plus metered APIs. Here every UA, AI crawlers included, gets the same full page and dossier. robots.txt names only ia_archiver, and the room-list JSON needs no key. The restriction lives entirely in the terms' blanket anti-scraping clause and in 429 rate limits. Robots-based compliance tooling will read the site as permissive, so production ingestion should go through the affiliate feed or a written grant.

The index is a presence index

The stream, the reason people visit, cannot be indexed after the fact. The useful retrieval question is "who matching X is live now", answered from a feed polled every few minutes. The durable layer is the broadcaster entity: name, gender, location, chat language, about-me, follower count, satisfaction score and broadcast history. Room subjects are a poor text field. They are tip-goal strings ("Goal 9: tip 350 … [5440 left]"); only 60% are pure ASCII, and emoji and token prices run through them. Hashtags are the real query vocabulary.

Identifier & dedup

The username is the URL key, and a separate 7-character room_uid/broadcaster_uid sits in the dossier. Key on the UID so the entity survives a rename. Language subdomains are hreflang alternates of the same room, so collapse them. The username is also the thumbnail key, which joins any feed row to its image without another call.

Sitemap = lagging active-broadcaster roster

The 728K list is a dated snapshot, not a full account dump. Its per-URL lastmod is the generation time, it misses 19% of today's live broadcasters, and about 85% of sampled entries had not broadcast in 30 days. Use it as a seed of known entities, never as a freshness signal. Join it with the live feed to classify entities as active, dormant or gone (401 banned).

Skew changes ranking

Viewers follow a power law (top 1% of rooms = 83%), while supply is geographically concentrated (Colombia = 43% of rooms, 10% of viewers). Ranking by viewer count surfaces a few dozen rooms. A diversity-aware index needs supply-side features (country, language, tags, newness: 206 rooms carried is_new) rather than popularity alone. Note that country and location are self-declared free text ("Antioquia, Colombia", "Europe") and blank for 32% of rooms.

Geo and identity gates

Broadcasters can block regions: 6 of 80 sampled bios returned "not available to your region or gender" from a US egress. Room lists and bios therefore differ by egress country, and an index built from one region silently drops region-blocked creators. Run feed polling from each target market.

Indexing Plan

  • Retrieval unit: the broadcaster entity (UID, username, gender, location, preferred chat language from the dossier, about-me text, tags seen, follower count, satisfaction score, last broadcast), plus a live-session record (start timestamp, subject, tags, viewers) that expires when the room goes offline.
  • Primary keys & joins: broadcaster_uid → username → thumbnail and room URL; hashtag → tag-table room and viewer counts; social_medias links → the same creator on fan platforms (cross-site entity resolution); photo-set ID → set metadata.
  • Ingestion paths, ranked:
    1. Affiliate online-rooms feed with a program wm code: the sanctioned live-state path. Coverage ceiling = all public rooms; rate limits open.
    2. The sitemap (no auth, 15 files, about 70MB uncompressed) as the entity seed, re-fetched when the index's lastmod moves.
    3. Bio and dossier JSON per entity only with written permission. Technically open, but the terms prohibit automated collection, and the room list 429s at 1 req/s. A full bio pass over 728K entities at the 2.5s pacing that avoided 429s would take about 21 days from one IP (calculated by Site Wise).
  • Recrawl cadence: live feed every 2–5 minutes; tag table every 15 minutes; entity bios for recently live broadcasters daily; the full sitemap roster monthly. Ban and region-block status on each touch.
  • Derivatives to generate: tag normalization (the tag table gives canonical lower-case forms); language ID on subjects and about-me text; per-entity activity histograms from session start times. No visual derivatives (no stored frames or thumbnails).
  • Demo to build: a supply-versus-demand board. Snapshot the room list hourly for a week and chart live rooms against viewers by country and hashtag, to show when Colombian supply peaks relative to US demand. Metadata only, no media.

Risks to the Index

  • Contract over code: the open JSON is not a licence. The terms ban automated collection outright, so the defensible footprint is the affiliate feed plus link-outs. Assume the SPA endpoints can change or gain auth without notice; they are undocumented internals.
  • Identifiable people in explicit content: performers are real, often pseudonymous, and their location fields and social links make re-identification possible. Store no images or video. Honour bans and region blocks, and make deletion propagation cover entities that disappear from both the feed and the sitemap.
  • Age and jurisdiction rules: age-verification laws (Texas HB 1181 and similar state laws) apply to anything that displays this content to users. A search surface over it inherits the same age-gating duties as the site.
  • Region-dependent corpus: creator-side geo-blocks mean no single crawl location sees the whole live set. Missing that skews any "complete" index.
