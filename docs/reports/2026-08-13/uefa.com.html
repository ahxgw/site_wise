Context

UEFA is the governing body of European football — an association of 55 national federations, not a company. It was founded 15 June 1954 in Basel and has been headquartered in Nyon since 1999. Its money comes almost entirely from selling broadcast and sponsorship rights to competitions it owns (Champions League, Europa League, Euro), then redistributing most of it to clubs and associations.

uefa.com is the owned-and-operated publishing surface for that portfolio: editorial news, match centres with live data, video clips, and regulatory documents across roughly 300 competitions. For a full-modal index it is unusual in two ways. It is a primary source of structured sports facts — fixtures, results, squads, stadiums — rather than a re-publisher, so its records are canonical rather than copies. And its technical and legal postures point in opposite directions: the crawl controls are among the most permissive of any site profiled here, the terms of use among the most restrictive.

Retrieval Notes (site-specific deltas)

The robots/terms contradiction

This is the defining fact about indexing uefa.com. robots.txt blocks no crawler, names no AI agent, and advertises six sitemaps — more permissive than nearly any major platform. Terms & Conditions §6.2 then prohibits systematically collecting content "to create a collection, database, or directory", bans "robots, spiders, or scripts", and explicitly forbids using content "to develop or train any software, model, algorithm, or AI tool, including machine learning systems or generative AI models". inferred The permissive robots.txt reflects neglect rather than a grant of rights. Basis: the file's Disallow list is entirely operational paths (/test/, /libraries/, /thirdparties/, preview tokens) with no content-policy rule of any kind, while the T&C carries a specific, current AI-training clause — the two were plainly written by different functions, and only one was updated for the AI era.

Identifier & dedup

Two schemes, both usable. Editorial content uses a four-part hex ID — 02a8-215538a51719-2eeabf96b5c7-1000 — appearing in the article URL and the editorial.uefa.com image path, so text and hero image join without heuristics. Matches use a plain integer identical in the sitemap slug and the API id: /match/2049220--lyon-vs-fenerbahce/ ↔ id: "2049220". The trailing slug is decorative; key on the ID.

Sitemaps are a window, not a census

Treat every sitemap count as a floor. Ten of 23 video sitemaps return exactly 200 URLs, 22 of 27 news sitemaps are empty, and three match sitemaps are byte-identical duplicates. An indexer that trusts the advertised 16,192 entries will simultaneously over-count (5.8% dupes) and badly under-count the real archive — the match API alone reaches back to 1994 while the match sitemap advertises 1,767 URLs.

Authoritative content field

Do not trust articleBody. It carries the standfirst — 100 characters on the sampled match report — not the article. The rendered HTML body is authoritative for text; JSON-LD is authoritative for date, genre, language and rights. For matches the reverse holds: the API record beats the page, because the page wraps the event in LiveBlogPosting whose liveBlogUpdate array was empty pre-kickoff.

Cross-modal joins

Match ID is the universal spine: it joins the match page, the API record, the stadium entity, both team entities, and referee assignments. Video is the weak link — clips carry their own editorial hex ID with no match ID reference in the sitemap or on the page, so associating a highlight with its fixture requires matching on title text and date. inferred No exposed video→match foreign key exists. Basis: the video: sitemap extension has seven fields, none of them an identifier, and the sampled video page emitted no JSON-LD beyond WebPage.

Language handling

Eight language subdomains exist (en, fr, de, es, it, pt, ru plus www as x-default), but hreflang is declared on exactly one of 10,285 root sitemap entries. Content IDs are stable across subdomains — fr.uefa.com serves the same path with a 200 — but the sampled article returned English copy there, so a 200 does not prove a translation exists. Verify per object; do not assume 8× the corpus. By contrast the API embeds a translations map directly in each record, which is the reliable multilingual surface.

Freshness & recrawl

Sharply bimodal and calendar-driven rather than continuous. Match records mutate by the second during a fixture and then freeze permanently; editorial is near-static once published; regulatory documents in the document sitemaps show lastmod values from 2019. Recrawl should follow the fixture calendar, which is itself published through the API — the site tells you when to come back.

Quality & spam

Effectively absent as a concern, and that is the point. Every object is first-party editorial or first-party competition data with copyrightHolder: UEFA and no UGC surface. This is a small, clean, high-authority corpus — the opposite of the ranking-manipulation profile that dominates the default assumptions.

Indexing Plan

  • Retrieval unit: two of them. The match (keyed on integer match ID, carrying teams, competition, stage, venue, officials, status, score) and the article (keyed on editorial hex ID, carrying headline, body, genre, date, hero image). Video attaches to neither cleanly and should be its own unit until a match join is established.
  • Primary keys & joins: match ID joins page ↔ API ↔ team ↔ stadium. Editorial hex ID joins article ↔ hero image. Competition ID (307 values) is the partition key for the whole corpus. Video → match is unsolved; see Open Questions.
  • Ingestion paths, ranked: 1) comp.uefa.com/v2 + match.uefa.com/v5 — no auth, 500/page, multilingual, archive to 1994; by far the best value, and the only path whose ceiling is pagination rather than discovery. 2) The video: sitemap extension — the only source of video duration and thumbnails; ceiling ~200/competition. 3) HTML crawl of editorial for article bodies, since JSON-LD will not supply them; ceiling is discovery, because the news sitemaps are mostly empty. 4) Nothing else — no RSS, no llms.txt, no oEmbed, no bulk dump.
  • Recrawl cadence: drive it off the fixture calendar pulled from the API. Live matches: seconds while status is in play. Upcoming fixtures: daily, since lineups and referees land late. Finished matches: once, then never. Editorial: daily on news/latest.xml. Documents: quarterly at most — several have not changed since 2019.
  • Derivatives to generate: ASR on video is the highest-value derivative here, because it is the only route to a video→match join (team and player names in the commentary) and the only way to make 2,971 clips searchable at all given the absence of transcripts. Article-level embeddings are worth it; image OCR is not, since the corpus is match photography rather than text-bearing graphics.
  • Demo to build: a match-ID resolver — paste any uefa.com match URL, strip the slug, and fan out to the competition and match APIs to render the full entity graph (both squads, stadium with postal address, referees, competition stage) from public endpoints alone. It makes the central claim of this report concrete: the richest data on this site is the part with no auth and no documentation.

Risks to the Index

  • The terms are the risk, not the tech. Every measured signal says this corpus is easy to take; §6.2 says taking it is prohibited, with an explicit AI-training ban. Ingestion beyond personal viewing needs a licence conversation before an engineering plan. Safest read: index for discovery and linking, do not redistribute, do not train.
  • The APIs are undocumented and so unversioned in practice. The v2 and v5 paths imply earlier versions are already gone. They exist to serve uefa.com's own front end and can be retired in a redesign without notice; the CORS preflight already advertises x-api-key, so the plumbing to close them is in place.
  • robots.txt is a lagging indicator. Against a T&C that names generative AI explicitly, the absence of any AI-crawler directive reads as an unclosed gap, not a settled position. Assume it closes; design ingestion that degrades gracefully.
  • Rights fragmentation on video. Match footage is licensed territory by territory to the broadcasters paying the €4.06B that funds the organisation — the modality most likely to be geo-fenced or withdrawn from third-party surfaces.
  • Sitemap defects mislead capacity planning. Identical match sitemaps and hard-capped video sitemaps make the advertised corpus wrong in both directions at once. Size the index off API pagination, not <loc> counts.

Open Questions

  • How large is the real match archive? The API returns 1994 fixtures but exposes no total. Closed by paginating match.uefa.com/v5/matches at limit=500 across all 307 competition IDs and every season year, then deduping on match ID.
  • Is there a licensed data feed, and on what terms? UEFA sells rights but publishes no developer portal. Closed by a direct enquiry to UEFA's media/marketing function, or by finding an existing licensee's attribution requirements.
  • Can video clips be joined to fixtures without ASR? Closed by sampling ~50 clips across competitions and testing whether any internal player configuration or undocumented endpoint carries a match ID.
  • How much of the corpus is genuinely translated across the seven language subdomains? Closed by fetching a fixed sample of article IDs on each subdomain and diffing og:title against the English original — a 200 alone proved insufficient in the probe.
  • What is uefa.com's actual traffic and its seasonality amplitude? No first-party figure is published, and match-driven sites peak violently. Closed by a panel estimate with the estimator named, or by UEFA disclosing digital reach in a future annual report.
  • Why are three women's match sitemaps byte-identical? Closed by re-probing after the next fixture round to establish whether it is a persistent generation bug or a transient deploy artefact.
