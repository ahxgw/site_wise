Context

Twitch is the West's dominant live-streaming platform, owned by Amazon since 2014 and monetized through channel subscriptions, Bits, advertising, and Prime bundling. Its primary corpus is ephemeral by construction: the flagship object is a live stream that exists for hours, then either expires or survives as a time-limited VOD. That inverts the usual indexing problem — the corpus is enormous in flow and small in stock, and the wall is contractual rather than technical. Twitch's robots.txt is unusually permissive, with no AI-crawler blocks at all, but its Developer Agreement caps caching of API data at twenty-four hours and forbids re-syndication outright. inferred Twitch does not defend its corpus by blocking crawlers; it defends it by making a durable third-party copy a contract breach. Basis: robots.txt (Probe Log) names zero AI user agents while the Developer Agreement's 24-hour cache clause and re-syndication ban bind every API consumer (Verified Metrics).

Retrieval Notes (site-specific deltas)

Only where Twitch differs from the default platform assumptions.

Identifier & dedup

The URL key (twitch.tv/<login>) is mutable — logins can be changed and later reclaimed by another account, so an index keyed on the visible URL silently re-attributes a channel's history after a rename. Only the numeric user ID is stable. Clips carry a further trap: the same clip is addressable on clips.twitch.tv/<slug> and twitch.tv/<login>/clip/<slug>, two canonical-looking URLs on different hosts.

Retrieval unit

There is no document. A live channel page is a slot whose contents are replaced every broadcast, so the useful unit is the broadcast session (channel × start time × category) and, below it, the transcript segment. Indexing twitch.tv/<login> as a page produces a record whose meaning expires within hours.

Cross-modal joins

Everything hangs off user_id. Clips carry video_id plus an offset — the one join anchoring a short-form object to its long-form parent, though the parent disappears when the VOD expires, orphaning the clip. Chat replay joins a VOD by second offset: the only text↔video alignment Twitch exposes.

Authoritative content field

Inverted relative to text platforms: the title and category are creator-set metadata, often written once and left stale for a six-hour stream, while the actual content lives in audio nobody transcribes. The most reliable text description of a Twitch broadcast is its chat log, not its title.

Freshness & recrawl

The contractual cache cap and the natural half-life of live objects happen to agree: a compliant index of live state is also a correct one. The mismatch is on the durable end — clips and channel entities are stable for years but may not legitimately be retained past a day without a commercial agreement.

Internal vs external search

robots.txt disallows /search, and on-site search covers channels and categories, not spoken content. Combined with the metadata-free shell (Probe Log), no search engine — internal or external — can answer "which stream discussed X". This is the largest unindexed spoken-word corpus in the top 100.

Indexing Plan

  • Retrieval unit: the broadcast session (channel × start time × category), with transcript segments and clips as child records; clips are the only child worth persisting.
  • Primary keys & joins: numeric user_id as the spine — never the login. video_id + offset joins clips and chat replay to their parent VOD; game_id joins sessions to the category tree.
  • Ingestion paths, ranked: 1) EventSub push subscriptions for stream online/offline and channel updates — the only way to catch sessions that never appear in any sitemap. 2) Helix polling of /streams and /clips within the 800-point bucket, for discovery and backfill. 3) Chat over IRC/WebSocket per tracked channel, as the de facto transcript. 4) The sitemap — 33.5K URLs, 4% of them placeholders, frozen since March; useful for the category tree and nothing else. 5) Public crawl — worthless, returns an identical empty shell to every agent.
  • Recrawl cadence: live state via push, never polling; category tree monthly; clip metadata weekly; channel entities on EventSub update events only.
  • Derivatives to generate: ASR over clip audio (clips are short, permanent, and legally embeddable — the best cost-per-minute target); chat-log summarization as a proxy transcript for live sessions; thumbnail embeddings for detecting the same game across channels.
  • Demo to build: a 24-hour-TTL compliant indexer — ingest Helix + chat into a store that hard-expires every record at 24 hours, and show what fraction of a useful search index survives the expiry. The interesting output is the gap between what you can retrieve and what you can keep.

Risks to the Index

  • The 24-hour cache cap is the whole strategy. A persistent Twitch index is not a crawling problem but a licensing one; without a commercial arrangement, retention past a day breaches the Developer Agreement regardless of how the data was obtained.
  • robots.txt and the ToS contradict each other. robots.txt permits generic crawlers on /directory and names no AI agents, while the ToS bans automated access "for any purpose". Compliance with the machine-readable signal is not a defence.
  • inferred The crawlable surface is unmaintained and may simply be withdrawn. Basis: the sitemap has not regenerated in 122 days, five of eight children are truncated at a ~9,999 cap, and 2,399 entries are unreplaced --redacted_channel-- placeholders (Probe Log) — signals of a pipeline nobody is watching.
  • inferred Amazon is the privileged crawler. Basis: robots.txt grants four Amazon-owned agents a bare Disallow: while every other agent faces ~40 blocked paths (Probe Log). Parity with first-party retrieval is not available to third parties on any published terms.
  • No oEmbed means no safe cheap display path. Both oEmbed endpoints 404 (Probe Log), so third-party UIs must use the iframe player with a parent allowlist — which couples display to your own domain registry and breaks for aggregators.

Open Questions

  • Total live catalogue size — how many VODs and clips actually exist, versus the 14,715 the sitemap advertises. Closeable by sweeping Helix /clips and /videos per game_id with an app token and summing paginated cursors.
  • Current VOD retention periods by account tier, and whether the 8,280 sitemap VOD URLs still resolve. Closeable with a batched Helix /videos?id= lookup over the sampled IDs — the SPA returns 200 for deleted objects, so HTTP status cannot answer it.
  • Chat message volume per day platform-wide — no published figure; closeable by sampling IRC joins across a stratified channel set and extrapolating by concurrency.
  • Twitch revenue and user counts — Amazon does not break Twitch out in segment reporting, so no official figure has been published since July 2014. Only an Amazon disclosure would close this.
  • Whether any commercial arrangement lifts the 24-hour cache cap, and on what terms. The agreement references "commercial arrangements described above" but publishes no rate card; closeable only by contacting Twitch developer relations.
  • Whether Twitch content is licensed for AI training to anyone, including Amazon. No published policy exists in robots.txt, the ToS, or the Developer Agreement.
